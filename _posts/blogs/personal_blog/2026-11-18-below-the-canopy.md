Airborne lidar can see the ground under trees. Laying a lidar terrain model next to aerial photos of the same place shows the shapes that the forest hides: banks, ditches, platforms and pits that the photos show only as treetops. These are the places where I think the lidar shows something built and old, and where I could not find any record of a site.

None of them has been checked on the ground yet. I am reporting them to the regional heritage services, so I give only the general areas here.

## Forêt de Châtillon, Burgundy

Five places in the forest between Villiers-le-Duc and Voulaines-les-Templiers, in the Côte-d'Or:

- **A quadrangular enclosure** 130 to 150 m across, with an inner rectangle of about 100 m. Its banks are low, broad and worn down. Inside are several round platforms or small mounds and a small rectangular building with a hollow, and faint field plots run off its south-east side. It looks like a Gallo-Roman farmstead.
- **A square enclosure** of about 95 m with broad, soft banks, a small mound inside and a sunken rectangle against its north side. Low banks nearby may be its fields.
- **An enclosure of about 100 × 75 m** with a small platform inside near its north side. A bank runs from its north-east corner to a long bank that runs north–south for about 300 m, so the enclosure sits inside a wider system of banks.
- **A field system** of low banks covering at least 900 × 600 m, with irregular parcels of 100 to 250 m and an angular enclosure of about 150 × 90 m.
- **An oval enclosure round a walled rectangle**, on the wooded plateau above Voulaines-les-Templiers, a village named after its Templar commandery. The oval bank and ditch measure about 105 × 165 m, and the rectangle inside, about 40 × 75 m, has divisions that look like walls. This one may be medieval rather than Roman.

At all five the aerial photos show only forest, and the banks ignore the straight forestry rides that cut through them. The forest is known for Gallo-Roman farms preserved under the trees, so some of these may already be on the regional archaeological map, which is not public.

## South of Raahe, Finland

Four tar pits in the forest between Raahe, Vihanti and Pyhäjoki, in North Ostrobothnia. Each is a ring with a raised rim round a central hollow, the shape of the pits where pine was burnt for tar. Ostrobothnia made tar for wooden ships for centuries.

- A ring about 12 m across with a hollow 0.6 m deep, alone on smooth ground. Its rim is drawn out on one side, probably where the tar ran out.
- A small, crisp ring about 9 m across with a hollow 0.75 m deep, on a low ridge that may be an old track or beach ridge.
- The largest, about 16 m across with a hollow 1.35 m deep. Its rim has a gap where the outlet was probably cut.
- A well-formed ring about 12 m across with a hollow about 1 m deep, next to a rock outcrop at the edge of a mire.

None of the four is in the Finnish register of ancient monuments.
